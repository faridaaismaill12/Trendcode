What is Post-Quantum Cryptography (and why should you care)?

Published Nov 9, 2026

Every time you log in, pay online or send a message, encryption keeps your data private. Most of it relies on math problems, like factoring huge numbers, that classical computers can't solve in any reasonable time.

The quantum problem

A large enough quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve those problems quickly, breaking the public-key encryption used today: RSA, ECC and Diffie-Hellman. (New to qubits? Start with our plain-English quantum explainer.)

Symmetric encryption such as AES is in much better shape. The best known quantum attack, Grover's algorithm, only halves its effective strength, so moving to 256-bit keys is enough.

Harvest now, decrypt later: attackers can steal encrypted data today and store it until quantum computers can unlock it. Data that must stay secret for years is already at risk.

The fix: new math

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) uses math problems believed to be hard for both classical and quantum computers. It runs on today's normal hardware, so no quantum computer is needed to use it.

In 2024, NIST published the first PQC standards:

  • ML-KEM (FIPS 203): for securely exchanging keys, based on lattices.
  • ML-DSA (FIPS 204): for digital signatures, also lattice-based.
  • SLH-DSA (FIPS 205): a hash-based signature scheme, used as a conservative backup.

In 2025 NIST also selected HQC, a key-exchange scheme built on different (code-based) math, as a backup in case a weakness is ever found in lattices.

Is anyone using it yet?

Yes. Major browsers and messaging apps already use hybrid key exchange, which combines a classical algorithm with ML-KEM so a connection stays secure as long as either one holds. If you use an up-to-date browser, some of your traffic is probably already post-quantum protected.

What should organizations do?

  • Inventory where cryptography is used across systems.
  • Prioritize long-lived sensitive data.
  • Build crypto-agility so algorithms can be swapped without rebuilding systems.
  • Test hybrid modes that combine classical and PQC algorithms during the transition.
  • Ask your vendors for their post-quantum roadmap, since much of your cryptography lives in products you buy.

The takeaway

Quantum computers that can break encryption aren't here yet, but migrating cryptography takes years. The time to start is now.
