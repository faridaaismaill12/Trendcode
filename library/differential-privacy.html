Differential privacy

A mathematical guarantee that a result barely changes whether or not your data is included.

🛡️ Privacy Intermediate 3 min read

01In plain words

Differential privacy (DP) is a precise promise: an analysis gives almost the same answer whether or not any single person's data is in the dataset. So nobody can learn much about you from the output, no matter what else they know. It works by adding carefully measured random noise to results. Our full explainer has an interactive coin-flip demo.

02Why it matters

Traditional anonymisation keeps failing against re-identification. DP is the first approach with a mathematical guarantee that holds even against attackers with unlimited outside knowledge. It's now used by national statistics offices and large technology companies, and increasingly to train AI models that can't memorise individuals.

03How it works

  1. Measure sensitivity: how much could one person change the answer? For a count, at most 1.
  2. Choose a privacy budget ε (epsilon): smaller ε means more noise and stronger privacy.
  3. Add calibrated noise: the Laplace or Gaussian mechanism adds random noise scaled to sensitivity ÷ ε.
  4. Track composition: every query spends some budget, and repeated queries add up, so systems cap the total.
  5. Central vs local: in central DP a trusted curator adds noise to results. In local DP each device randomises its own data before sending it, as in randomized response.

04Real-world example

The census and your phone

The 2020 US Census used differential privacy to protect published statistics. Apple uses local DP to learn things like popular emoji and words across devices, and Google's RAPPOR (2014) used it in Chrome to collect usage statistics. Libraries like OpenDP make DP available to researchers.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • A provable, quantifiable guarantee, not just a hope.
  • Robust to attackers with outside information.
  • Composes: you can reason about total privacy loss across many releases.

Limitations

  • Accuracy cost, especially for small groups or rare events.
  • Choosing ε is a policy decision with no universal answer.
  • The budget runs out: you can only answer so many questions.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Implementation details matter. Floating-point quirks and badly generated randomness have broken DP implementations.
  • Report the ε actually used. Very large ε values offer little real protection.
  • DP protects individuals, not groups. Aggregate findings about communities can still be sensitive.
