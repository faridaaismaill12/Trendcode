DNA computing

Using molecules of DNA to store data and even perform calculations.

⚡ Emerging Advanced 3 min read

01In plain words

DNA is nature's information storage: four chemical “letters” (A, C, G, T) encoding the instructions for life. DNA computing uses those molecules as a computer. Strands can represent data, and chemical reactions that join or cut strands can perform calculations, with trillions of molecules working in parallel in a single test tube.

02Why it matters

DNA is astonishingly dense and durable. In theory a few grams could store the world's data, and it can last thousands of years if kept cool and dry. With global data growing explosively, DNA storage is a serious candidate for long-term archives. Molecular computing could also enable “smart” medical diagnostics that compute inside the body.

03How it works

  1. Encode: binary data is translated into sequences of A, C, G and T, with error-correcting codes added.
  2. Synthesise: machines build the DNA strands chemically.
  3. Compute (optional): designed strands bind to each other according to base-pairing rules. Mixing them explores many possible solutions at once, as in Leonard Adleman's 1994 experiment that solved a small path-finding problem.
  4. Store: DNA is kept dry and cool, needing no power.
  5. Read: DNA sequencing reads the strands back, and the decoder reconstructs the original data.

04Real-world example

Archiving files in molecules

In 2016, Microsoft and the University of Washington stored and retrieved about 200 MB of data in DNA, including a music video. In 2019 they demonstrated an automated end-to-end system that wrote and read the word “hello”. Research labs also build DNA logic circuits that detect combinations of molecules, a step toward programmable diagnostics.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Extreme data density and very long lifetime.
  • No power needed to keep stored data.
  • Massive parallelism for certain search problems.
  • DNA won't become obsolete: we'll always be able to read it.

Limitations

  • Slow: writing and reading take hours to days.
  • Synthesis and sequencing are still expensive.
  • Error-prone chemistry needs heavy error correction.
  • Not a general-purpose replacement for electronic computers.

06Security & privacy implications

  • In 2017, researchers encoded a software exploit in synthetic DNA that compromised the sequencing software reading it, a reminder that data from any source is untrusted input.
  • Long-lived archives must stay encrypted: data secret today may need to stay secret for centuries.
  • DNA synthesis raises biosecurity questions, so synthesis providers screen orders for dangerous sequences.
