Digital identity

How people and organisations prove who they are online, and how to do it without over-sharing.

🛡️ Privacy Beginner 3 min read

01In plain words

Offline, you prove who you are with a face, a signature or an ID card. Online, a digital identity is the collection of identifiers (usernames, email addresses, account numbers), credentials (passwords, passkeys, digital documents) and attributes (name, age, address) that represent you to websites, employers, banks and governments.

02Why it matters

Almost everything online depends on knowing who someone is, or at least what they're allowed to do: banking, healthcare, voting, work systems. Weak identity leads to fraud and account takeovers. Over-collected identity leads to surveillance and data breaches. Getting identity right is foundational to both security and privacy.

03How it works

  1. Identity proofing: establishing that a person is who they claim to be, for example by checking a passport once when opening a bank account.
  2. Authentication: proving it's still you each time, with passwords, one-time codes or, better, phishing-resistant passkeys.
  3. Authorisation: deciding what you're allowed to do once you're identified.
  4. Federation: “Sign in with…” buttons use standards like OAuth 2.0, OpenID Connect and SAML so one identity provider vouches for you to many services.
  5. Digital wallets and verifiable credentials: issuers (a government, a university) sign credentials that you keep in a wallet on your phone and present to others, sometimes revealing only one attribute, such as “over 18”.

04Real-world example

National e-IDs and mobile wallets

Estonia's digital ID lets citizens sign documents, file taxes and vote online. India's Aadhaar has enrolled well over a billion people. Under its updated eIDAS regulation, the EU requires member states to offer citizens a European Digital Identity Wallet, and mobile driving licences built on the ISO/IEC 18013-5 standard are rolling out in several countries.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Convenient, fast access to services from anywhere.
  • Strong credentials such as passkeys dramatically reduce phishing and account takeover.
  • Selective disclosure can reveal less than physical ID cards do.

Limitations

  • Centralised identity systems are high-value targets and potential single points of failure.
  • People without smartphones, documents or connectivity can be excluded.
  • Account recovery when credentials are lost remains hard.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Linking all activity to one identifier enables tracking across services. Unlinkable, pairwise identifiers and minimal disclosure help.
  • Identity providers must be protected like critical infrastructure, because a breach there compromises every service that trusts them.
  • Prefer phishing-resistant authentication and make recovery processes as strong as sign-in, since attackers target the weakest path.
