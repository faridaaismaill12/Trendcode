Brain-computer interfaces

Systems that read brain signals and turn them into commands, letting people control devices with thought.

⚡ Emerging Intermediate 3 min read

01In plain words

Every thought and movement involves electrical activity in your brain. A brain-computer interface (BCI) listens to that activity, through sensors on the scalp or tiny electrodes implanted in the brain, and uses software to translate patterns into actions: moving a cursor, typing words, or controlling a robotic arm.

02Why it matters

For people with paralysis, ALS or locked-in syndrome, BCIs can restore communication and independence. Research systems have let people type, speak through a synthesised voice and control devices again. Longer term, BCIs raise profound questions about human augmentation and mental privacy.

03How it works

  1. Record: non-invasive BCIs use EEG caps on the scalp (safe but noisy). Invasive ones use electrode arrays on or in the brain (precise but need surgery). Some, like stent-based designs, are placed through blood vessels.
  2. Process: filter noise and extract features from the signals.
  3. Decode: machine-learning models map neural patterns to intended actions, such as “move left”, a letter, or a speech sound.
  4. Act and give feedback: the device responds, and the user sees the result, so brain and decoder adapt to each other over time.

04Real-world example

Typing and speaking by thought

The BrainGate research trials have let people with paralysis control cursors and robotic arms. In 2023, teams at Stanford and UCSF demonstrated speech neuroprostheses decoding attempted speech into text at roughly 60–80 words per minute. In 2024, Neuralink's first patient used an implant to play chess and browse with a cursor controlled by thought.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Can restore communication and mobility to people who have lost them.
  • Rapid progress thanks to better electrodes and AI decoders.
  • Non-invasive versions are already used for research, neurofeedback and some gaming.

Limitations

  • Implants require brain surgery, with risks of infection and signal degradation over time.
  • Non-invasive signals are weak and noisy, which limits speed and accuracy.
  • Expensive, and still mostly in clinical trials.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Neural data is arguably the most intimate data there is. It could reveal intentions, emotions or health conditions.
  • A compromised implant could be a safety issue, not just a privacy one, so medical-device security standards apply.
  • Laws are starting to respond: Chile amended its constitution in 2021 to protect “neurorights”, and Colorado passed a 2024 law adding neural data to its privacy protections.
