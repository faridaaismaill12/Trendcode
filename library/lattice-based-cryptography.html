Lattice-based cryptography

The geometry of high-dimensional grids that underpins today's main post-quantum standards.

🔐 Cryptography Advanced 3 min read

01In plain words

Picture an endless grid of dots, like an orchard planted in perfect rows. That's a lattice. In two dimensions it's easy to find the dot closest to any spot. Now imagine a grid in 500 or 1,000 dimensions, slightly skewed, with a bit of random “noise” added. Finding the nearest point becomes astronomically hard, for classical and quantum computers. Lattice-based cryptography hides secrets in that difficulty.

02Why it matters

Lattices are the foundation of ML-KEM and ML-DSA, the main NIST post-quantum standards, so they'll soon protect most internet traffic. They're also the engine behind fully homomorphic encryption. Understanding lattices means understanding where cryptography is heading for the next few decades.

03How it works

  1. Hard problems. Security rests on problems like the Shortest Vector Problem and, above all, Learning With Errors (LWE), introduced by Oded Regev in 2005: given many noisy linear equations, recover the secret.
  2. Add noise. Without noise, LWE is just linear algebra and easy to solve. A small random error on each equation makes it hard, and solving it is provably as hard as worst-case lattice problems.
  3. Add structure for speed. “Module” and “ring” variants arrange the numbers as polynomials, shrinking keys and speeding up operations, often with fast transforms similar to the FFT.
  4. Build schemes. A public key is a set of noisy equations; encryption adds more noise around the message; only the private key can strip the noise away. Signatures use similar tricks with careful “rejection sampling” so they don't leak the key.

04Real-world example

ML-KEM in your browser

When Chrome connects to a site that supports it, the TLS handshake includes an ML-KEM-768 key share, a lattice-based KEM, alongside a classical X25519 share. The lattice part adds roughly 1–2 KB to the handshake and a fraction of a millisecond of computation.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Believed secure against quantum computers, with strong theoretical foundations (worst-case to average-case reductions).
  • Fast, often faster than RSA, and suitable for constrained devices.
  • Versatile: KEMs, signatures, identity-based encryption and fully homomorphic encryption all come from lattices.

Limitations

  • Larger keys and ciphertexts than elliptic-curve cryptography.
  • Parameter selection is subtle, and concrete security estimates keep being refined.
  • Some schemes have a tiny probability of decryption failure, which must be engineered to be negligible.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Constant-time implementations are vital. The “KyberSlash” timing issues found in several Kyber implementations in 2023–24 showed how division instructions can leak secrets.
  • Heavy reliance on one mathematical family is a concentration risk, which is why code-based and hash-based alternatives are also being standardised.
