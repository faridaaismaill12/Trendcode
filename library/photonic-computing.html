Photonic computing

Using light instead of electricity to move and process information faster and with less heat.

⚡ Emerging Advanced 3 min read

01In plain words

Today's chips push electrons through tiny copper wires, which generates heat and limits speed. Photonic computing uses particles of light, photons, instead. Light travels fast, different colours can share one channel without interfering, and light beams can be combined to do certain maths almost instantly.

02Why it matters

AI workloads are dominated by enormous matrix multiplications, and data centres are hitting limits on power and heat. Light can carry data between chips with far less energy (it already does in fibre networks), and optical circuits can perform some AI calculations very efficiently. Photonics is also one of the leading approaches to building quantum computers.

03How it works

  1. Optical interconnects: lasers and photodetectors move data between chips and servers as light. This is already widespread inside data centres.
  2. Integrated photonics: waveguides, modulators and detectors are fabricated on silicon chips, much like electronic circuits.
  3. Computing with interference: meshes of tunable interferometers combine light beams so the output intensities equal the result of a matrix multiplication, the core operation of neural networks.
  4. Hybrid systems: electronics still handle memory, control and non-linear steps, with light doing the heavy linear algebra.

04Real-world example

Light in the data centre

Every large data centre already relies on optical transceivers to link servers, and chip makers are developing “co-packaged optics” that bring light right up to the processor. Start-ups such as Lightmatter have built photonic interconnect and accelerator chips for AI, and companies including PsiQuantum and Xanadu are building quantum computers based on photons.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Very high bandwidth and low latency.
  • Potentially much lower energy per operation for linear algebra.
  • Less heat than pushing current through dense wiring.

Limitations

  • Non-linear operations and memory are hard to do with light alone.
  • Converting between electrical and optical signals costs energy and time.
  • Analogue optical computing has limited precision and is sensitive to noise and temperature.
  • Manufacturing is less mature than conventional silicon electronics.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Optical links can be tapped physically. Fibre isn't inherently confidential, so encrypt data in transit.
  • New hardware means new supply chains and new side channels, which need the same scrutiny as electronic chips.
  • Photonic quantum computers, if they reach scale, are part of why the post-quantum migration matters.
