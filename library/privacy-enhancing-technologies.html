Privacy-enhancing technologies

The toolkit of techniques that let us use data while revealing as little as possible about people.

🛡️ Privacy Beginner 3 min read

01In plain words

Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) are an umbrella term for methods that protect personal data while still letting it be useful. Some hide individuals in statistics, some let computers work on data they can't see, and some let you prove facts without revealing the details. Think of them as different tools for the same job: getting value from data without exposing people.

02Why it matters

Organisations want to analyse data, train AI and collaborate. People want and deserve privacy, and laws like the GDPR require data protection by design. PETs reduce that conflict. They're increasingly used in healthcare research, advertising measurement, finance and government statistics.

03How it works

  1. Hide individuals in the data: anonymisation, pseudonymisation, aggregation and differential privacy (calibrated noise).
  2. Compute without seeing: homomorphic encryption (compute on ciphertext), secure multi-party computation (parties jointly compute a result without revealing their inputs), and trusted execution environments (hardware-isolated processing).
  3. Keep data where it is: federated learning and federated analytics send the computation to the data.
  4. Prove without revealing: zero-knowledge proofs and selective disclosure credentials.
  5. Replace real data: synthetic data generated to mimic statistical patterns without being real records.

04Real-world example

COVID-19 exposure notifications

The Apple–Google exposure notification system (2020) let phones exchange rotating random Bluetooth identifiers. If someone tested positive, their recent identifiers were published, and every other phone checked for matches locally. Health authorities learned nothing about who met whom, a deliberate privacy-by-design choice that combined several PET ideas.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Unlocks data use that would otherwise be impossible or illegal.
  • Reduces the damage if data is breached.
  • Builds trust with users and supports compliance.

Limitations

  • Each technique has costs in accuracy, speed or complexity.
  • Requires specialist expertise to configure correctly.
  • Easy to misapply, giving a false sense of privacy (“privacy theatre”).

06Security & privacy implications

  • PETs protect specific things against specific threats. Always state the threat model: who is being protected from whom?
  • Combining PETs, for example federated learning with secure aggregation and differential privacy, usually gives stronger protection than any one alone.
  • PETs don't replace basic security: access control, encryption and data minimisation still matter.
