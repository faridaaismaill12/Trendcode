Neuromorphic computing

Chips that compute more like a brain, with spiking neurons, events and very little energy.

⚡ Emerging Intermediate 3 min read

01In plain words

Your brain runs on about 20 watts, less than a light bulb, yet it recognises faces and walks through crowds effortlessly. Conventional chips burn far more power shuttling data between separate memory and processors. Neuromorphic computing builds hardware inspired by the brain: lots of simple “neurons” that hold their own memory and only fire short electrical spikes when something changes.

02Why it matters

AI is hungry for energy, and many devices like sensors, wearables, drones and robots need intelligence without a big battery or a cloud connection. Neuromorphic chips promise dramatic efficiency gains for always-on, real-time tasks, and they're a research path to understanding how brains compute.

03How it works

  1. Spiking neurons: instead of passing continuous numbers, artificial neurons accumulate input and emit a spike when they cross a threshold, as biological neurons do.
  2. Event-driven: nothing computes unless a spike arrives, so idle parts of the chip use almost no power.
  3. Memory next to compute: synaptic weights are stored right beside each neuron, avoiding the costly memory traffic of conventional chips.
  4. Learning: networks are trained by converting conventional neural networks or with brain-inspired rules such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity.
  5. Sensors that match: “event cameras” report only pixels that change, feeding spikes directly into neuromorphic processors.

04Real-world example

Loihi, TrueNorth and Hala Point

IBM's TrueNorth (2014) packed a million spiking neurons onto a chip drawing tens of milliwatts. Intel's Loihi research chips followed, and in 2024 Intel unveiled Hala Point, a system with about 1.15 billion neurons, for research into efficient AI. Practical demos include gesture recognition, odour detection and low-power robotic control.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Potentially orders-of-magnitude better energy efficiency for suitable tasks.
  • Very low latency for real-time sensing.
  • Naturally suited to streaming, sparse, time-based data.

Limitations

  • Software tools and programming models are immature compared with GPUs.
  • Training spiking networks is harder, and accuracy can lag conventional deep learning.
  • Mostly research hardware today, with limited commercial availability.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Running AI locally on low-power devices can improve privacy, since raw sensor data never leaves the device.
  • New hardware brings new, little-studied attack surfaces such as power and timing side channels.
  • As with any edge device, physical access and firmware updates need protecting.
