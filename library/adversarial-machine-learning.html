Adversarial machine learning

How carefully crafted inputs can fool AI models, and how to make models more robust.

🤖 AI Advanced 3 min read

01In plain words

Show a well-trained image classifier a photo of a panda and it says “panda”. Now change the pixels by an amount too small for any human to notice, and it confidently says “gibbon”. That's an adversarial example. Adversarial machine learning studies these attacks, which exploit how models see the world differently from us, and the defences against them.

02Why it matters

AI increasingly makes decisions in the physical and security world: reading road signs, detecting malware, filtering spam, recognising faces. If small, deliberate changes can flip those decisions, attackers can evade detection or cause accidents. Robustness is a safety requirement, not just an academic curiosity.

03How it works

  1. Evasion attacks modify inputs at test time to cause a wrong answer. The classic Fast Gradient Sign Method (Goodfellow et al., 2014) nudges every pixel slightly in the direction that most increases the model's error.
  2. White-box vs black-box: with access to the model's internals, attackers compute gradients directly. Without it, they probe with queries or craft attacks on a substitute model that often transfer.
  3. Physical attacks survive the real world: printed patches, stickers or 3D objects designed to fool cameras from many angles.
  4. Other families include poisoning training data, extracting the model, and inferring private training data.
  5. Defences include adversarial training (training on attacked examples), input preprocessing, ensembles, and certified robustness methods that prove no small perturbation can change the answer.

04Real-world example

Stickers on a stop sign

In 2018, researchers showed that a few black-and-white stickers placed on a real stop sign could make a road-sign classifier read it as a speed-limit sign from most viewing angles. Another team 3D-printed a turtle that a leading image classifier identified as a rifle. Both are reminders that “accurate on the test set” isn't the same as “safe under attack”.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Understanding attacks leads to more robust, better-generalising models.
  • Adversarial testing is now a standard part of AI red-teaming.
  • Certified defences can give mathematical guarantees for small perturbations.

Limitations

  • Robustness often costs accuracy and compute.
  • Many published defences were later broken. It's an ongoing arms race.
  • Guarantees usually cover only narrow, mathematically defined perturbations, not every real-world trick.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Assume attackers will probe any model exposed through an API. Limit query rates and don't return more detail (such as raw confidence scores) than needed.
  • For high-stakes uses, combine models with independent checks and human review instead of trusting a single prediction.
