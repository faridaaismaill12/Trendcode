Supply chain security

Protecting software and hardware from attacks that sneak in through trusted suppliers, dependencies and build systems.

🛡️ Privacy Intermediate 3 min read

01In plain words

Modern software is assembled, not written from scratch. A typical app pulls in hundreds of open-source packages, uses cloud services and ships through automated build pipelines. A supply chain attack compromises one of those trusted ingredients, so the victim installs the attack themselves, signed and delivered through official updates.

02Why it matters

One compromised component can reach thousands of organisations at once, which makes the supply chain extremely attractive to attackers. Recent years have seen some of the most damaging incidents ever travel this way, prompting new regulations and industry frameworks.

03How it works

  1. Know your ingredients: keep a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) listing every component and version.
  2. Vet and pin dependencies: use trusted sources, pin versions with lock files and checksums, and scan for known vulnerabilities.
  3. Secure the build: isolated, hardened CI/CD pipelines with least privilege, reproducible builds and provenance records. The SLSA framework defines levels.
  4. Sign everything: sign artefacts and verify signatures before deploying. Sigstore makes this easier for open source.
  5. Assess suppliers: require security practices from vendors, and plan for how you'd respond if one is compromised.

04Real-world example

SolarWinds, Log4Shell and xz

In 2020, attackers compromised SolarWinds' build system and slipped a backdoor into Orion updates, which about 18,000 customers downloaded. In December 2021, the Log4Shell vulnerability in the ubiquitous Log4j library exposed countless systems at once. In 2024, a backdoor carefully planted over years in the xz compression library (CVE-2024-3094) was caught just before it reached major Linux distributions, spotted by an engineer investigating a half-second slowdown in SSH logins.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • SBOMs make it possible to answer “are we affected?” within hours instead of weeks.
  • Signing and provenance make tampering detectable.
  • Many defences are automatable in CI pipelines.

Limitations

  • Deep dependency trees are hard to fully audit.
  • Open-source maintainers are often unpaid volunteers with limited resources.
  • Vendor assessments can be checkbox exercises rather than real assurance.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Watch for typosquatting (look-alike package names) and dependency confusion, where public packages impersonate private internal ones.
  • Protect build systems and signing keys as carefully as production. Compromising them compromises everything downstream.
  • Supporting the open-source maintainers you depend on is itself a security investment.
