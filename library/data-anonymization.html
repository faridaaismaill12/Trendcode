Data anonymization

Removing the link between data and the people it describes, and why that's much harder than it sounds.

🛡️ Privacy Beginner 3 min read

01In plain words

Anonymisation means transforming data so it can no longer be tied back to an individual. The obvious first step is deleting names. The trouble is that other details, like postcode, birth date, the films you rated or the places you go, can act as a fingerprint. Real anonymisation means making sure nobody can re-identify people, even by combining the data with other sources.

02Why it matters

Anonymised data can be shared and analysed with far fewer legal restrictions. Under the GDPR, truly anonymous data isn't personal data at all. That makes anonymisation hugely valuable for research, open data and analytics, but getting it wrong has exposed millions of people.

03How it works

  1. Remove direct identifiers: names, email addresses, ID numbers.
  2. Generalise: replace exact values with ranges (age 34 → 30–39, a full postcode → its first part).
  3. Suppress: remove rare records or values that stand out.
  4. k-anonymity: ensure every combination of identifying attributes is shared by at least k people (Sweeney, 2002). Refinements like l-diversity also make sure sensitive values vary within each group.
  5. Add noise or aggregate: publish statistics rather than records, or use differential privacy for formal guarantees.
  6. Pseudonymisation is not anonymisation: replacing names with codes is reversible with the key, so the data is still personal data.

04Real-world example

Search logs and taxi rides

In 2006, AOL released “anonymised” search logs. Within days, journalists identified user No. 4417749 from her searches. In 2014, New York City released taxi trip data with hashed medallion numbers, and because there are few possible medallions, the hashes were quickly reversed, exposing drivers' incomes and, combined with photos, some celebrities' trips.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Enables data sharing for research and public benefit.
  • Reduces legal obligations and breach impact when done properly.
  • Simple techniques are cheap and widely understood.

Limitations

  • Re-identification risk is real and grows as more data becomes public.
  • Stronger anonymisation usually means less useful data.
  • Hard to prove a dataset is truly anonymous, especially rich data like location traces.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Assume attackers have auxiliary data. Linkage attacks combine datasets to single people out.
  • Hashing identifiers isn't anonymisation when the possible values can be guessed or enumerated.
  • For high-risk releases, prefer aggregate statistics with differential privacy, or controlled access environments, over publishing records.
