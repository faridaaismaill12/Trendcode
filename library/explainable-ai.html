Explainable AI

Techniques that help people understand why an AI system made a particular decision.

🤖 AI Intermediate 3 min read

01In plain words

If a bank's AI rejects your loan, “computer says no” isn't good enough. You want to know why. Explainable AI (XAI) is the set of methods that open up the black box: which factors mattered most, what would have changed the outcome, and whether the model is reasoning in a sensible way at all.

02Why it matters

Explanations build trust, help experts catch mistakes (like a medical model that learned to spot hospital watermarks instead of disease), make bias visible, and are often legally required. In the US, lenders must give specific reasons for denying credit, and EU law gives people rights around automated decisions.

03How it works

  1. Interpretable by design: simple models such as decision trees, rule lists or linear models whose reasoning can be read directly.
  2. Feature attribution: SHAP assigns each input feature a share of the prediction using Shapley values from game theory, and LIME fits a simple local model around one prediction.
  3. Saliency maps: for images, highlight which pixels most influenced the output.
  4. Counterfactuals: describe the smallest change that would flip the decision, for example “approved if income were £3,000 higher”.
  5. Global explanations: summarise overall model behaviour, such as feature importance across all decisions.

04Real-world example

Explaining a loan decision

A credit model rejects an application. A SHAP explanation shows the top factors were high existing debt and a short credit history, while postcode had almost no influence. The lender uses this to write the legally required reason notice, and its fairness team checks that no protected characteristic, or an obvious proxy for one, is driving decisions.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Helps catch errors, shortcuts and bias before they cause harm.
  • Supports regulatory compliance and user trust.
  • Gives domain experts a way to check and improve models.

Limitations

  • Post-hoc explanations are approximations and can be unfaithful to what the model really does.
  • Explanations can be manipulated: researchers showed that biased models can be built to fool LIME and SHAP into looking fair.
  • There's often a trade-off between the most accurate models and the most interpretable ones.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Detailed explanations can leak information about training data and make it easier to copy (extract) the model.
  • Attackers can use explanations to find exactly which features to change to game a system.
  • Balance transparency with these risks, for example by giving detailed explanations to auditors and simpler reasons to the public.
