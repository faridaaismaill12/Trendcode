Quantum key distribution

Using the physics of single photons to share a secret key, where any eavesdropping leaves detectable traces.

🔐 Cryptography Intermediate 3 min read

01In plain words

Imagine sending a secret by writing it on soap bubbles: anyone who touches a bubble pops it, so you'd know someone was snooping. Quantum key distribution (QKD) works in a similar spirit. Two parties send single particles of light (photons) to each other, and because measuring a quantum state disturbs it, an eavesdropper can't copy the photons without introducing errors the two parties can spot. The result is a shared secret key they can use with ordinary encryption.

02Why it matters

Most of today's key exchange relies on maths problems that a large quantum computer could solve. QKD takes a completely different route: its security rests on the laws of physics rather than on a problem being hard. That makes it attractive for links that must stay secret for decades, such as government or bank backbones, and it's one of the first practical quantum technologies.

03How it works

  1. Send: Alice sends Bob a stream of photons, encoding each bit in one of two randomly chosen “bases” (for example, polarisation that is either straight or diagonal). This is the BB84 protocol, proposed by Bennett and Brassard in 1984.
  2. Measure: Bob measures each photon in a basis he also picks at random. When his basis matches Alice's, he reads the right bit; when it doesn't, his result is random.
  3. Sift: Over an ordinary (but authenticated) channel they compare which bases they used, never the bits themselves, and keep only the matching positions.
  4. Check for spies: They reveal a sample of bits. An eavesdropper who measured photons in transit would have disturbed them, pushing the error rate up. If it's too high, they throw the key away.
  5. Clean up: Error correction fixes remaining mismatches, and privacy amplification shrinks the key to squeeze out anything an eavesdropper might have partially learned.

04Real-world example

Satellite QKD between continents

In 2017, China's Micius satellite distributed quantum keys between ground stations in China and Austria, and the keys were used for an intercontinental video call. Earlier, in 2007, Geneva used commercial QKD equipment to protect the link carrying election results. Several countries now run metropolitan QKD networks over fibre.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Security is based on physics, not on an unproven assumption that some maths problem is hard.
  • Eavesdropping is detectable while the key is being created, not years later.
  • Keys are “future-proof”: a future quantum computer can't go back and break a properly generated QKD key.

Limitations

  • Distance is limited (roughly 100 km over fibre) without “trusted relay” nodes, which must themselves be secured.
  • Needs specialised, expensive hardware and dedicated fibre or line-of-sight links. It can't run over normal internet connections.
  • QKD doesn't authenticate who you're talking to. You still need classical authentication, often pre-shared keys or signatures.
  • Real devices have been hacked through implementation flaws (for example, blinding the detectors with bright light), even though the theory is sound.

06Security & privacy implications

  • The security proof covers the protocol, not the hardware. Side-channel attacks on detectors and lasers are the main practical risk, so certification and careful engineering matter.
  • Trusted relays break end-to-end guarantees: whoever controls a relay node can read the key.
  • Security agencies including the US NSA, the UK NCSC and France's ANSSI currently recommend post-quantum cryptography over QKD for most uses, mainly because of cost, authentication and implementation concerns.
