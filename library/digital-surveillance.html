Digital surveillance

How governments, companies and individuals monitor people through digital technology, and the protections against it.

🛡️ Privacy Beginner 3 min read

01In plain words

Digital surveillance is the monitoring of people's behaviour, communications or movements using digital tools. It ranges from targeted, court-approved wiretaps to mass collection of phone records, from cookies that follow you across websites to apps that sell your location, and spyware secretly installed on a partner's phone.

02Why it matters

Surveillance shapes power: who knows what about whom. It can help solve crimes and protect national security, but unchecked it chills free speech, enables discrimination and persecution, and creates huge stores of data that can be breached or misused. Understanding it is key to informed debate and to protecting yourself.

03How it works

  1. Communications and metadata: who contacted whom, when and from where. Metadata alone reveals relationships and routines.
  2. Commercial tracking: cookies, device fingerprinting, SDKs in apps and data brokers build detailed profiles, often shared through real-time ad auctions.
  3. Location data: phones, cell towers, Wi-Fi and licence-plate readers map movements.
  4. Biometrics: facial recognition in public spaces and online photo scraping.
  5. Spyware: commercial tools that compromise phones, sometimes with no click at all.

04Real-world example

From PRISM to Pegasus

In 2013, documents leaked by Edward Snowden revealed large-scale US and allied surveillance programmes, including PRISM. Investigations since 2021 have documented NSO Group's Pegasus spyware being used against journalists and activists via “zero-click” exploits. Clearview AI scraped billions of online photos for a facial-recognition database and has been fined by several European data-protection authorities.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Lawful, targeted surveillance can help investigate serious crime and terrorism.
  • Monitoring can improve public safety and system security when proportionate and overseen.

Limitations

  • Mass surveillance invades the privacy of huge numbers of innocent people.
  • A chilling effect on speech, journalism and political activity.
  • Collected data can be breached, misused or repurposed.
  • Facial recognition and profiling can be biased against particular groups.

06Security & privacy implications

  • End-to-end encryption, keeping phones updated and browser tracking protection are strong everyday defences.
  • Data minimisation is a security measure too: data that isn't collected can't be stolen or subpoenaed.
  • Legal safeguards matter. In Carpenter v. United States (2018), the US Supreme Court ruled that police generally need a warrant to obtain historical cell-phone location records, and the GDPR limits commercial tracking in Europe.
