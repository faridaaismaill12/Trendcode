Data poisoning

Corrupting the data an AI learns from to break it, bias it, or plant a hidden backdoor.

🤖 AI Intermediate 3 min read

01In plain words

A model learns whatever its data teaches it, including lessons someone slipped in on purpose. Data poisoning means tampering with training data so the resulting model misbehaves. Sometimes it's obvious, like making the model generally worse. Sometimes it's sneaky: a hidden backdoor where the model behaves perfectly until it sees a secret trigger.

02Why it matters

Modern AI is trained on huge datasets scraped from the web, contributed by users, or bought from third parties, far too large for anyone to check by hand. That makes poisoning cheap and hard to detect, and its effects can surface long after training, in spam filters, malware detectors, content moderation or language models.

03How it works

  1. Availability attacks inject noisy or mislabelled data to reduce overall accuracy.
  2. Targeted attacks make the model misclassify specific inputs while behaving normally otherwise.
  3. Backdoor attacks pair a trigger (a small patch, a rare phrase) with the attacker's chosen output, so the model learns “when you see this, do that”.
  4. Delivery routes include editing web pages that will be scraped, buying expired domains referenced in datasets, contributing to open datasets, or feeding malicious inputs to systems that learn online from users.

04Real-world example

From Tay to $60 of poisoned web data

In 2016, Microsoft's chatbot Tay learned from its conversations and was manipulated by users into posting offensive content within a day. In 2023, researchers showed that poisoning web-scale training sets is practical: by buying expired domains whose URLs appeared in popular image datasets, they could control a measurable fraction of the data for about $60. Tools like Nightshade now let artists deliberately “poison” their images against unauthorised training.

05Advantages & limitations

Advantages

  • Understanding poisoning pushes teams toward better data provenance and curation.
  • Backdoor-style techniques can be used defensively, for example as watermarks to prove a dataset was used.

Limitations

  • Poisoned data can be a tiny fraction of the total and still be effective.
  • Backdoors are hard to detect because the model looks normal on standard tests.
  • Retraining from scratch after discovering poison is expensive.

06Security & privacy implications

  • Track data provenance: know where every dataset came from, and pin datasets with cryptographic hashes so they can't change silently.
  • Filter and audit data with outlier and duplicate detection, and be very careful with systems that learn continuously from user input.
  • Test models for backdoors and monitor behaviour after deployment.
