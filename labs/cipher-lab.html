Crack the Cipher

For a thousand years, secret messages were protected by shifting letters around. Here you'll use that trick, then break it the way codebreakers did: by counting.

Beginner~10 minNot completed yet

1. Encrypt a message

A Caesar cipher shifts every letter the same number of places along the alphabet. Julius Caesar reportedly used a shift of 3.

Ciphertext

2. Brute force it

There are only 25 possible shifts. An attacker doesn't need to be clever. They can just try them all.

    3. Crack the intercepted message

    You've intercepted this message. Slide your guess until the violet English-frequency markers line up with the cyan bars from the ciphertext. In English, E, T and A are the most common letters, and a shift can't hide that.

    Decrypted with your guess

    What this teaches about modern crypto

    • Small key spaces die to brute force. 25 keys takes milliseconds. AES-256 has 2256 keys, more than the number of atoms in the observable universe.
    • Patterns leak. The Caesar cipher kept the shape of English intact. Modern ciphers are designed so ciphertext is indistinguishable from random noise. No letter counts, no patterns.
    • Security must not depend on the method being secret. Everyone knows how AES and ML-KEM work. Only the key is secret. This is called Kerckhoffs's principle.
